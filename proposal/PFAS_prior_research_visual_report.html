What the existing evidence says

Prior research already supports associations between serum PFAS and several liver enzymes and cholesterol measures. It also shows that effects differ by compound, outcome, population, and model. The open question for this proposal is not simply whether PFAS correlates with health biomarkers, but whether relationships learned in NHANES reproduce in an independently characterized exposed Florida community.

Most repeatableAST, GGT, ALP, total cholesterol, and LDL/non-HDL signals.
More variableALT, bilirubin, HDL, triglycerides, and fibrosis indices.
Least settledClinical fatty-liver/steatosis outcomes and causal interpretation.
Interpretation rule: most human results below are observational and cross-sectional. They support association and prediction, not proof that PFAS caused the biomarker changes.

Liver-function evidence

1. PFAS mixture and liver enzymes — Canadian Health Measures Survey

Borghese et al., 2022 · nationally representative cross-sectional analysis

Mixture model

Reported result

A one-quartile joint mixture increase was associated with 7.5% higher AST (95% CI 4.0–10.4), 9.7% higher GGT (1.7–17.0), and 2.8% higher ALP (0.5–5.4). No consistent association was observed with ALT or total bilirubin.

Proposal implication

PFAS mixtures and these liver enzymes have already been analyzed with quantile g-computation. Repeating the same analysis is not novel; external validation and transportability are more defensible contributions.

Source: Borghese et al., Environmental Health (2022). Figure reproduced under the article’s open-access license.

2. PFAS mixture weights and liver outcomes — NHANES 2009–2018

Yan et al., 2024 · 7,484 U.S. adults

WQS / NHANES

Reported result

The positive WQS mixture index was associated with ALT, AST, GGT, and total bilirubin. It was not statistically significant for FIB-4 (p=0.146). PFNA received the largest weight for ALT (0.757), AST (0.657), GGT (0.461), and bilirubin (0.648); PFOS received the largest FIB-4 weight.

How to read it

A large WQS weight means the chemical contributed strongly within this constrained mixture model. It does not by itself prove that the chemical is uniquely causal or more toxic.

Source: Yan et al., Journal of Biomedical Research (2024). CC BY 4.0.

3. Nonlinear exposure–response patterns — NHANES 2009–2018

Yan et al., 2024 · restricted cubic splines

Nonlinearity

Reported result

Examples include inverted-U patterns for PFOA–ALT and PFHxS with ALT/AST/GGT, a U-shaped PFNA–GGT relationship, and positive dose-response patterns for PFOA/PFNA with FIB-4.

Proposal implication

Assuming a single linear slope may miss structure. Nonlinear modeling is justified, but it is not itself new. Curves must also be checked for stability in the tails and in the Florida cohort.

Source: Yan et al. (2024). CC BY 4.0.

Reported conclusion

Higher ALT was associated with PFOA, PFOS, and PFNA; PFOA was also associated with higher AST and GGT. Rodent evidence consistently supported liver injury and steatosis signals.

Important limitation

Heterogeneity prevented a conventional pooled effect estimate. The synthesis establishes a signal but does not provide one universal dose–response coefficient for prediction.

Source: Costello et al., Environmental Health Perspectives (2022). EHP content is public domain unless otherwise noted.

Lipid and lipoprotein evidence

5. PFAS and cholesterol across lipoprotein subfractions

Haug et al., 2023 · POEM cohort, Sweden · 493 adults

Detailed lipid profile

Reported result

L-PFOS, PFOA, and PFDA were positively associated with cholesterol in multiple fractions, especially IDL and small, medium, and large LDL. PFHxS showed no significant association with the 13 cholesterol subfractions.

Proposal implication

“Lipids” should not be treated as one outcome. Total cholesterol, LDL/non-HDL, HDL, and triglycerides may show materially different relationships.

Source: Haug et al., Journal of Exposure Science & Environmental Epidemiology (2023). CC BY 4.0.

6. The triglyceride contrast

Haug et al., 2023 · same cohort and models

Mostly null

Reported result

Evidence for PFAS associations with triglycerides across 13 lipoprotein subfractions was weak to null, in marked contrast to the cholesterol results.

Proposal implication

Triglycerides remain worth testing, but the primary lipid hypothesis should probably emphasize total cholesterol and LDL/non-HDL rather than expecting uniform effects across all lipid outcomes.

Source: Haug et al. (2023). CC BY 4.0.

Why it matters

This study is conceptually closer to the proposed Florida cohort than NHANES: it examined a community with documented drinking-water contamination and measured serum PFAS plus clinical biomarkers.

What remains open

A community-specific association does not establish that an NHANES-trained model will be calibrated locally. That transportability question remains central to the proposal.

Source: Hall et al., Science of the Total Environment (2023).

Overall conclusion for the proposal

Existing research has already established the analytic pipeline serum PFAS → individual/mixture modeling → liver and lipid biomarkers. NHANES, WQS, q-gComputation, restricted cubic splines, and related methods have all been used. The proposal should therefore avoid presenting any of those elements alone as the innovation.

Learn PFAS–biomarker relationships in harmonized NHANES
→
Externally test discrimination and calibration in the Florida cohort
→
Identify transport failure and adapt with limited local data

The strongest proposed contribution: determine whether nationally learned PFAS–liver and PFAS–lipid relationships remain reliable in a specifically exposed U.S. community, and develop a principled recalibration/adaptation strategy when they do not.

Recommended endpoint hierarchy

  • Primary liver: ALT, AST, GGT, ALP.
  • Primary lipids: total cholesterol, LDL, and/or non-HDL.
  • Secondary: bilirubin, HDL, triglycerides, FIB-4 or other prespecified fibrosis indices.
  • Exploratory: NAFLD/steatosis definitions and subgroup-specific nonlinear effects.
